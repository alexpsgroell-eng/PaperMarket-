      01

      What a share actually is

      Ownership, not a bet

      A share is a tiny slice of a company. Buy one and you own that slice: a claim on part of the profits (paid out as dividends or reinvested to grow the business) and usually a vote at the annual meeting. Apple has around 15 billion shares, so one share is a very small slice, but it is real ownership rather than a wager on a number.

      The price is simply the last figure a buyer and a seller agreed on. It moves when people change their minds about the company's future profits. Over years, prices tend to follow those profits. Over days, they follow mood, headlines and guesswork, which is why a stock can drop 3% on a day when nothing happened to the company at all.

      02

      Index funds, and why the grey line matters

      500 companies in one purchase

      The S&P 500 is a list of about 500 of the largest companies in the United States, weighted by size, so Apple and Microsoft count for far more than a regional bank. Nobody can buy the list itself, but a fund can buy every company on it and sell you a slice of the whole bundle. VOO is one such fund: one purchase and you own all 500.

      That is why the grey line sits in every chart here. It is the benchmark, the result you would have got by doing the simplest possible thing. "Beating the market" means staying above that line. Most professional fund managers fail to do it over ten or more years once fees are counted, which is why a plain index fund is the usual starting point.

      03

      Risk and return move together

      Bigger swings are the price of bigger gains

      Volatility is how much a price swings. In the simulator BND barely moves, VOO drifts, NVDA and TSLA lurch, and MEME is a coin toss. Each is modelled on how the real thing tends to behave.

      Higher expected returns almost always come packaged with bigger swings. The catch is that a big swing down can arrive exactly when you need the money. A common rule of thumb: money you will need within five years does not belong in anything that can halve.

      04

      Diversification is the only free lunch

      Spread the risk you are not paid to take

      Hold one company and a single bad earnings call can take a fifth off your portfolio overnight. Hold hundreds across different industries and the individual disasters mostly cancel out, while the general growth of business stays. You get a smoother ride without giving up much return, which is why economists call it the only free lunch in investing.

      It does not protect you from everything. When the whole market falls, nearly everything falls with it. What it removes is the risk that one company's mistake wipes you out.

      05

      Compounding

      Growth on top of growth, with sliders

      Compounding is growth on top of growth. Your money earns a return, and next year that return earns a return of its own. Early on the effect is boring. After a couple of decades it is most of the money. Drag the sliders and watch the gap open between investing and simply saving.

      Invested at 7%Kept as cash

      Real markets do not return the same figure every year, so treat the rate as a long-run average rather than a promise. The S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% a year over the past century before inflation, and around 7% after it.

      06

      Dollar-cost averaging

      Same amount, same day, every month

      Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, say $100 on the first of every month, whatever the price is doing. When prices are high your $100 buys fewer shares; when they are low it buys more. You stop asking "is now a good time?" because the answer is always "it's the first of the month".

      This is how most people invest in practice: a standing order on payday into a broad fund. It will not beat a perfectly timed lump sum, but nobody times lump sums perfectly, and it keeps you from freezing when the market looks scary.

      07

      Time in the market beats timing it

      The best days hide next to the worst ones

      Nobody reliably predicts short-term moves, including the people paid to try. Markets fall 10% or more at some point in most years, and the strongest days tend to cluster right after the worst ones, so investors who sell in a panic usually miss the rebound. Missing just a handful of the best days across a couple of decades cuts the final result dramatically.

      Since the 1920s the S&P 500, with dividends reinvested, has not lost money over any 20-year stretch, even though plenty of brutal years sit inside those windows. Past results guarantee nothing about the future, but the lesson has been consistent: how long you stay invested matters more than the moment you start.

      08

      Fees and taxes

      Small percentages, large sums

      A 1% annual fee sounds like nothing. Over 30 years it removes roughly a fifth to a quarter of what you would otherwise finish with, because it is charged on the growth as well as the original money. Index ETFs typically charge between 0.03% and 0.2% a year. Actively managed funds often charge 0.5% to 1.5% for results that are usually worse.

      Taxes work the same way in reverse. Most countries offer accounts where investment growth is not taxed, such as a Stocks and Shares ISA in the UK (or a Junior ISA for under-18s) and a Roth IRA in the US. Filling one of those before touching a plain brokerage account is one of the few decisions that is almost always right.

      09

      Mistakes beginners make

      The simulator lets you make them for free
      • Selling after a drop because it feels unbearable, which locks the loss in.
      • Buying whatever everyone is talking about, after it has already risen.
      • Checking the app ten times a day. More looking leads to more trading, and more trading tends to lower returns.
      • Borrowing money to invest, or using leverage. Losses get amplified just as much as gains.
      • Investing money you will need soon.
      • Confusing a great company with a great investment. Price matters: a brilliant business bought at too high a price can still lose you money.
      • Believing anyone who promises guaranteed returns. Nobody honest can.
      10

      Before you invest real money

      A checklist, in order
      • An emergency fund first: a few months of expenses sitting in cash.
      • No expensive debt (credit cards, overdrafts) running alongside your investments.
      • A horizon of at least five years, ideally much longer.
      • Only money you could lose entirely without it changing your life.
      • Understand what you own. If you cannot explain it in one sentence, do not buy it.
      • Start boring, small and regular: a broad index fund, a fixed amount, every month.
      • If you are under 18 you usually cannot open an account yourself, but a parent or guardian can open a junior account in your name. Until then this simulator costs nothing to practise with.
      11

      Glossary

      The words you will keep seeing
      Ticker
      The short code a stock trades under, like AAPL or VOO.
      Share
      One unit of ownership in a company.
      ETF
      Exchange-traded fund: a basket of investments you buy and sell like a single share.
      Index
      A list of stocks used to measure a market, such as the S&P 500 or the FTSE 100.
      Benchmark
      The index you compare your results against. The grey line.
      Dividend
      Cash a company pays out to shareholders from its profits.
      Volatility
      How much a price swings. High volatility means big moves both ways.
      Bull / bear market
      A long rise / a fall of 20% or more from a peak.
      Drawdown
      How far a price has fallen from its highest point.
      Market cap
      Share price times number of shares: what the whole company is valued at.
      P/E ratio
      Share price divided by yearly profit per share. A rough gauge of how expensive a stock is.
      Portfolio
      Everything you hold, taken together.
      Expense ratio
      A fund's yearly fee as a percentage of your money.
      Liquidity
      How easily something can be sold for cash at a fair price.
